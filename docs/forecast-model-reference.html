01How the whole thing works

One idea powers the whole system: find past days that looked like today, and average what you sold on them. Everything else — the confidence badge, the restock warning, the accuracy gauge — is built on top of that single act of comparison.

1
Describe today
The system builds a fingerprint of today: which weekday it is, whether it is a weekend or a Philippine holiday, and how much demand the last 7, 14 and 30 days averaged, plus whether demand is rising or falling. That fingerprint is 7 numbers — the independent variables.
2
Find the look-alikes
Every past sales day for that egg size — up to 365 days of history — gets the same fingerprint. Each one is compared with today using a distance formula, and the closest few are kept. Nothing is scored on days after the day being predicted.
3
Average them, weighted
The nearest k days are averaged. Closer days count more (1/√distance), and older days fade further (e^(−daysAgo/τ)). The model picks k itself by trying 3, 5, 7 and 9 and keeping whichever scores best in a walk-forward test.
4
Convert to eggs, money, and a confidence band
The tray average is rounded once, then multiplied by that size's tray capacity to get eggs. Money is forecast by a second, independent pass over the same days. A 90% bootstrap band is drawn around the tray number, and a confidence level is assigned from how many past days were available.
5
Compare against the shelf and give a verdict
Expected eggs are subtracted from eggs actually in stock. The result decides one of three verdicts — Restock now, Order more, or Enough in stock — and separately decides whether a low-stock alert is raised. Separately again, each tray gets an Urgency tag from its expiry date.
The single most important idea

The forecast and the stock check are two different questions. The forecast asks “how many eggs will I sell today?” The verdict asks “do I have that many?” A high forecast with low stock produces Restock now; a low forecast with plenty of stock produces Enough in stock. Neither number alone means anything.

Every figure quoted in this document is read directly from the source files listed in Section 08. Where the code and its own comments disagree, the disagreement is recorded in Section 09.

03Independent variables — the inputs

These are the things the model is allowed to look at. They are the independent variables: if you changed one of them, the forecast would change. There are exactly seven, declared in one place, and no others are used.

The complete list

day_of_week  ·  is_weekend  ·  is_holiday  ·  demand_7d  ·  demand_14d  ·  demand_30d  ·  demand_trend

Critically: no inventory variable is an input. Trays in stock, expiry dates and quantity levels are deliberately excluded from the forecast so that the model predicts demand, never supply. Stock only enters afterwards, when forming the verdict.

3.1  Calendar variables

day_of_week
Which day of the week is it? For an egg shop, Saturday is not Tuesday.

PHP's date('w'), giving 0 = Sunday through 6 = Saturday. This is usually the single strongest signal: weekends carry heavier retail traffic than midweek.

It is stored in the database as 1–7 using DAYOFWEEK(), which subtracts 1 to realign it with the 0–6 scale the model uses.

Range 0–6  ·  Type: integer  ·  Source: target date
is_weekend
Is today Saturday or Sunday?

Simply 1 when day_of_week is 0 or 6, otherwise 0. This is deliberately redundant with day_of_week — it is a derived convenience flag that makes the weekend effect explicit in the distance calculation rather than something the model has to infer from seven separate numeric distances.

Two features describing the same fact is a mild form of double-counting. It is kept because the normalised distance for day_of_week treats “Sunday to Tuesday” as merely two steps away, which understates how different they really are.

Range 0–1  ·  Type: integer  ·  Derived from day_of_week
is_holiday
Is today a Philippine public holiday?

Flag 1 if the date appears in the generated holiday list, otherwise 0. The list has two parts:

  • 9 fixed dates — 1 Jan, 9 Apr, 1 May, 12 Jun, 21 Aug, 1 Nov, 30 Nov, 25 Dec, 30 Dec.
  • 3 movable dates — Maundy Thursday, Good Friday and Easter Sunday, all derived from a computable Easter algorithm rather than a hard-coded calendar, so the dates are always right.

Holiday egg sales behave differently enough to be worth flagging separately from “weekend”.

Range 0–1  ·  Type: integer  ·  Source: functions.php:170

3.2  Demand-window variables

All three use the same query: the average trays sold per recorded day over a window that ends strictly before the target date. Two details matter. First, the window is date < target, so today's partial sales never leak into today's forecast. Second, it averages over recorded days, not calendar days — so one quiet Tuesday does not drag the average down as hard as it would under a calendar-day denominator. With COALESCE(..., 0), a size with no rows in the window returns 0 rather than NULL.

demand_7d
On a typical recent day, how much did I sell?

Mean trays_sold across the previous 7 days. This is the model's main sense of current demand level and typically carries the most weight in the distance calculation, because it is the hardest of the seven features to accidentally match.

Unit: trays/day  ·  Type: float  ·  Window: 7 days
demand_14d
What about the last two weeks?

The same average over 14 days. Where demand_7d is a fast read, this one is steadier and partly smooths out a single unusual day. Where the two disagree, demand is either climbing or falling — which is what the trend feature formalises.

Unit: trays/day  ·  Type: float  ·  Window: 14 days
demand_30d
And over a full month?

The 30-day average. This is the slowest and most stable of the three. It is what keeps a fortnight of unusually good trading from being read as a permanent new normal — a past day whose 30-day demand looks nothing like the current month will correctly rank as a poor match.

Unit: trays/day  ·  Type: float  ·  Window: 30 days
demand_trend
Is demand rising or falling? This week versus the week before.

Not a raw figure but a difference:

demand_trend = demand_7d − average(trays sold over days 8–14 ago)

Positive means this recent week beat the one before; negative means it fell short. Including the slope as well as the level lets the model prefer a rising Tuesday over a flat one even when both average the same number of trays.

Unit: trays/day difference  ·  Type: float  ·  Can be negative

3.3  How the seven become a prediction

The seven features are only comparable because they are put on a common scale first. For every training set, each feature's own minimum and maximum are recorded and every value is rescaled to fall between 0 and 1. Without this, day_of_week (0–6) would dominate the arithmetic simply by being numerically larger than is_holiday (0–1).

Distance & neighbour selection

Each training day's normalised fingerprint is compared with today's by straight-line (Euclidean) distance across all seven features — the square root of the summed squared gaps. Days are then sorted by distance and the nearest k are kept.

Weighting & averaging

Each kept day gets a weight of 1/√distance, multiplied by a recency factor of e^(−daysAgo / τ). The prediction is the weighted mean of those days' sales.

Why 1/√distance instead of the textbook 1/distance

Plain 1/d collapses almost all the weight onto the single nearest day, so k quietly behaves like “nearest 1” with extra steps and the vote of the other neighbours is lost. 1/√d still ranks nearer days first but lets the whole k window contribute. It is a deliberate departure from the textbook formula.

04Dependent variables — the outputs

These are what the model produces. They depend on the seven inputs plus k. Unlike the inputs, these are the numbers you actually see and act on.

4.1  The two primary targets

The model is run twice over the same days and the same neighbours, once for each target. The two runs are independent — revenue is never calculated by multiplying a tray count by today's price.

TargetFitted fromOutput
actual_demand sales_history.trays_sold How many trays of this size will sell today.
actual_sales sales_history.total_amount How much money this size will take today.
Why forecast revenue separately?

Price changes over time and revenue per tray is not constant. Running a second pass over the same historical days lets the model learn what revenue each size actually returned. The consequence is deliberate and documented on the page: predicted sales will not exactly equal eggs × today's price, and that is expected rather than a bug.

The rounding rule that keeps totals honest

Each size's tray figure is rounded once, then summed into its category total, then summed into the day total. Previously each size was truncated to an integer as it was added up, which lost up to a tray per size and made the visible totals disagree with each other. Eggs are always trays × capacity, so the two figures can never contradict each other.

The most important structural decision

Forecast and stock are kept strictly apart. A bug in stock tracking can therefore never silently corrupt the model, and the backtest scores can never be inflated by the model's own output being written back as “actual”. This separation is what makes the accuracy figures trustworthy.

06Evaluation variables

These are also dependent variables — they depend on the model's predictions — but they are computed afterwards, by replaying the forecast over real past days. They tell you how much the whole system is worth trusting.

Scored by backtest, not by reading stored rows

Metrics come from replaying the forecast across past days, where each day is predicted using only days before it. The stored knn_predictions table is never used as the scoring source, because it can be seeded, stale, or partly written by a cron job — and because the model itself writes to it. Anything less than 5 scored days returns an empty result set.

6.2  Directional metrics bias, not closeness

MetricFormulaWhat it means
precision((total − over_predicted) ÷ total) × 100Guards your cash. Share of days the model did not over-predict. Over-forecasting ties up money in stock that then ages.
recall((total − under_predicted) ÷ total) × 100Guards your sales. Share of days the model reached at least the actual figure. Under-forecasting turns customers away.
f1_score2 × (P × R) ÷ (P + R)Harmonic mean. Falls sharply when either direction is unhealthy, which is why it is used for the headline gauge.
These are not classical precision and recall

There is no true/false-positive matrix here. Both figures reduce to the sign of (predicted − actual) against a ±0.5-tray cut, so they measure directional bias, not classification quality. They are still useful — a model that is consistently 2 trays high will score badly on precision even if its average error is small — but the conventional textbook definitions do not apply. The page states this explicitly in its own footnote.

Why the headline gauge uses F1, not accuracy

Accuracy on its own is misleading here, because the ±1-tray tolerance is generous. A model that is systematically short can post a very high accuracy while its recall sits near 50%. F1 falls when either direction is unhealthy, so it is the safer single number to headline. Accuracy is still reported alongside it.

09Caveats & inconsistencies

Three places where the code does not fully agree with itself. None breaks the system today, but each one is worth knowing before a number is treated as authoritative.

1  Two different confidence-band rules exist

The canonical getConfidenceLevel() uses ≥30 → High, ≥10 → Medium, else Low, and this is what the live forecast and the Sure column use.

A separate inline rule elsewhere in the same file uses ≥30 → High, ≥14 → Medium, else Low when writing confidence during retraining.

Effect: a size with 10–13 history days can display Medium on the forecast page while having been persisted as Low. The displayed value is the more generous of the two. The two should be reconciled to one shared function.

2  Two different recency decay constants exist

The live prediction path decays by e^(−daysAgo/30), giving a 30-day time constant. The backtest path uses e^(−daysAgo/τ) with τ = 45, overridable via setRecencyTau().

Effect: the live forecast and the backtest that grades it weight recency differently. The reported accuracy therefore measures a slightly different model from the one that produces the daily number. Using one constant in both places would make the score strictly honest.

3  Precision and recall are sign tests, not classification scores

As covered in Section 06.2, both reduce to the sign of the error against a ±0.5-tray cut. A model that over-predicts every single day by one tray would score 0% precision while having a perfectly respectable hit rate — which is informative, but is not what the textbook terms imply to anyone reading the table without the footnote.

Recommendation: keep the metrics, but consider renaming them to “Did not over-predict” / “Did not under-predict” everywhere, so the label and the maths agree.

Lower-risk observations

  • is_weekend duplicates day_of_week. Both contribute to the distance, so weekends are effectively weighted double. Defensible as an explicit interaction term, but it is undocumented as one.
  • No minimum sample count for metricStatus() or the gauge bands. A size with 5 scored days at 100% shows the same green as one with 200 scored days at 90%. The sample_count ≥ 5 filter exists for model-health alerts but not for the on-page metric rows.
  • Forecast page shows one global staleness banner but per-size confidence. These are two separate mechanisms. The banner is global (any sales activity at all), the downgrade is per size. The page correctly avoids double-counting, but the two can disagree on screen.
  • Low Stock compares against a 7-day average forecast when a size had no sales, which can alert on a size the model itself rates Low confidence. The message does not surface the confidence level.
What the design gets right

Three decisions are worth protecting in any future change, because each one fixes a real failure mode:

  1. Forecast and stock are fully separated. Stock levels never enter the model, so inventory bugs cannot silently corrupt demand predictions.
  2. Metrics are scored by walk-forward backtest, not from stored rows. The model can never be graded against a figure it helped produce.
  3. Confidence is downgraded on staleness, not just sample count. Plenty of old data is treated as worse than a modest amount of fresh data, which is the honest relationship.
